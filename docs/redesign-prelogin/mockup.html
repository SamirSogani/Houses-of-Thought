The method

Seven layers, one house.

Every question moves through the same seven layers — Frame, Perspectives, Evidence, Assumptions, Conclusion, Implications, Review. Hover a room to see where it sits on the drawing.

  1. 1
    The Blueprint
    Frame

    Concepts, purpose, and the one question the house exists to answer — defined before anything argues.

  2. 2
    The Windows
    Perspectives

    Sub-questions and points of view — the same house, a different view from each.

  3. 3
    The Materials Yard
    Evidence

    Information and facts, cited — Research Mode can only cite what it actually retrieved.

  4. 4
    The Foundation
    Assumptions

    What has to be true for the reasoning to hold — including the unknown unknowns.

  5. 5
    The Wiring
    Conclusion

    The inference from evidence and assumptions to a claim — inspectable, not asserted.

  6. 6
    The Weather
    Implications

    What follows if the conclusion holds — sorted positive, negative, and uncertain.

  7. 7
    The Inspection
    Review

    Scored on Evidence, Logic, and Coverage, then stress-tested before it's called finished.

Layer 7 · Review

Scored, then attacked, before it's called finished.

A house isn't finished when the conclusion is written — it's finished once it's been stress-tested and scored. The Review layer is what makes "AI that guides, not decides" more than a slogan.

Practical / Logical

Focuses on what actually works in a classroom — time saved, skills gained, whether the tools are reliable enough to trust with real coursework.

Q. Does it save teachers time without creating new grading blind spots?

Yes for routine tasks like first-draft feedback; no for judgment calls about a specific student's growth — that still needs a teacher's eyes.

ConcludesAI earns a place on the routine, low-stakes tasks; the judgment calls stay human.

Emotional / Personal

Focuses on how it feels to learn with AI in the room — trust, motivation, and whether students still feel ownership over their own work.

Q. Do students feel like the work is still theirs?

Only when AI stays a sparring partner, not an author — students handed finished answers report feeling less invested, not more confident.

ConcludesOwnership survives AI involvement only if the AI visibly never writes the final answer.

Long-Term / Strategic

Focuses on what students need for a world where AI is already everywhere, and whether avoiding it now creates a bigger gap later.

Q. What happens to students who never learn to work alongside AI?

They enter a workforce already assuming AI fluency — the risk isn't AI in schools, it's schools being the only place it isn't encountered critically.

ConcludesThe long-term case favors structured exposure now over avoidance.

Assumptions

FoundationalThat "AI in schools" means one consistent thing, when a citation-checker and an essay-writer raise completely different questions.
Evidence-basedThat teachers have time to learn and supervise new tools — not guaranteed without dedicated training time.
Unknown unknownWhat happens to assessment once AI is in the room isn't fully known — most current policy is reactive, not tested.

There's no single answer to "should AI be used in schools" — it depends entirely on which task, and whether the AI stays a sparring partner instead of becoming the author.

Key tradeoffs

  • Time saved on routine tasks vs. new blind spots in grading
  • Preparing students for AI-fluent work vs. protecting the struggle where real learning happens

Strongest tension

The practical case for AI and the emotional case against it point in opposite directions on the exact same tasks.

Sit with this

If a tool could do the assignment for a student, is the assignment still teaching the thing you actually wanted them to learn?
